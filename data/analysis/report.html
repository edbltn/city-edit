The previous top-20 pooled 3½ years of crashes. That was the right way to find chronically bad intersections — and the wrong way to decide where to hang a poster today, because the city keeps responding to exactly this data. This audit re-pulls the full NYPD Motor Vehicle Collisions feed, keeps every crash since 2023 that injured or killed a pedestrian or cyclist, snaps each one to a named street intersection in City Edit's own walk graph (so every ranked intersection deep-links into the app), and re-ranks under six different definitions of "dangerous."

1. The old top-20 is stale — measurably

Re-rank the PDF's top 20 on the last 12 months only and just three stay in the citywide top 50. Chrystie St / Grand St — #7 all-time — recorded 3 crashes in the past year against a historical pace of ~8/yr and falls to #504. 8th Ave / W 57th St falls to #1048. Meanwhile E Fordham Rd / Webster Ave more than doubled its own baseline. Pooled rankings memorialize where danger was; the freshness-weighted rankings below track where it is.

2. Six definitions of "most dangerous"

"Dangerous" is a modeling choice, and different choices target different posters. Each definition below is computed for every intersection; the tabs show the citywide top 10 under each.

A Chronic (the PDF's definition)

Raw count of ped/cyclist injury-or-death crashes, 2023 → now. Finds the persistent offenders; blind to fixes.

B Fresh & severity-weighted

Last 12 months only, injured + 8×killed. The headline "most dangerous right now" number. The 8× is a choice — deaths dominate but a single fatal crash can't be outrun by two injuries.

C Recency-decayed

Every crash counts, weighted 0.5^(age/12mo). Smoother than a hard 12-month cut; a fix shows up as a fading score within months. Recommended default for poster placement.

D Mode-specific (ped / cyclist)

Definitions B–C restricted to pedestrian or cyclist victims. The two maps barely overlap — cyclist danger lives on bridge approaches and avenue bike routes, pedestrian danger on wide crossing distances.

E Heating up (trend outlier)

Poisson surprise: how improbable is the last-12-month count given the intersection's own 2023→mid-2025 rate (+½ pseudo-crash prior)? Catches emerging hotspots a pooled rank won't see for years.

F After dark

Share of crashes between 9pm and 6am (among intersections with ≥4 fresh crashes). A different intervention story: lighting, visibility, late-night speeds.

3. Outliers: who's escaping their own history

Every intersection with real signal, plotted against itself: historical pace (x) vs. the last 12 months (y). On the dashed line, nothing changed. Above it and improbably so (Poisson surprise ≥ 3, i.e. p < 0.001): heating up. Far below with a big baseline: cooling — several of these sit exactly where DOT shipped redesigns, which is the clearest evidence in the data that the city does respond and stale rankings do go stale.

5. Method & honest caveats

Join. 47,965 crash rows pulled (Socrata h9gi-nx95); 2,251 lacked usable coordinates; 5,611 fell >40 m from any named intersection (mostly mid-block) and are excluded; 40,103 snapped to 16,253 intersections. Intersections are nodes in City Edit's NYC walk graph where ≥2 distinct street names meet — the same graph the app serves, so poster QR coordinates resolve to selectable nodes. Weights. The 8× death weight and 12-month half-life are dials, not truths; rankings above are shown under multiple settings precisely so the choice is visible. Denominators. No exposure normalization — a Midtown intersection sees 100× the foot traffic of a residential one, so these are counts of harm, not per-crossing risk. That's the right metric for "where should a poster hang," the wrong one for "which design is most defective." Data lag. NYPD publishes with ~6-week delay; "last 12 months" means through June 11, 2026. Late-filed reports can still top up recent months slightly. Underreporting. Cyclist-only falls and near-misses never enter this dataset — which is exactly the gap the posters ask people to fill in City Edit.
